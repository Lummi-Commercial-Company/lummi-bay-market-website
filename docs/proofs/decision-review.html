How to use this page. The decisions are grouped into six blocks, A to F. Each block can be approved, questioned or sent back on its own — you do not have to take or leave all twenty-eight. Two entries are marked FROM THE BUILD rather than with a decision number: those were not decided in advance, they were forced by writing the code, and they are shown here because each one constrains what can be changed later.

Every entry says three things: what was decided, why, and what it costs — the constraint you are accepting along with the decision. Where a number appears it was measured in a browser, not estimated. The full reasoning for any entry is in docs/adr/ under the number shown.

What changed since 17 September

One thing changed, and it is the large one: the site was built. Every decision below was already settled when the first line was written, so this section is not a list of new decisions — it is the report on what happened when twenty-eight of them met a compiler. Three of them cost more than the record said they would, and those are annotated in place. None of them had to be reversed.

What exists now

Eleven pages, the layout shell they all sit in, the fuel price block, the data layer, and the CMS schema staff will edit through. Three separate checks pass on every commit: the type checker, the Next.js production build, and TinaCMS's own schema validation — which is the one that reads every content file back against the shape the CMS expects, and the only one that would catch a field an editor could break.

Pages live in the build11 — Home, Locations index, three Location pages, Truck Stop, Rewards, Fuel Prices, About, Contact, Privacy
Application code5,580 lines across 52 files, plus a 1,033-line CMS schema
Build result18 of 18 pages generated, no errors, no warnings suppressed
Decisions reversed to make it workNone — the record held

What is not built. Promotions, info pages and the tenants page exist in the CMS schema but nothing renders them yet — that is the Day 9 row in the plan below, and it is the honest gap in this report. The scheduled things that are built are the three that change what a visitor sees without anyone pressing publish: the emergency notice, the holiday hours override, and the footer social row.

Two things the build caught that no amount of reviewing would have

The privacy policy was one save away from being deleted. TinaCMS needs a field explicitly marked as a document's body text. The page collections were first written without one — which does not fail, it discards: the CMS would have opened /privacy showing everything except the roughly four thousand words of policy, and the next save would have written the file back without them. It was found before an editor ever opened the page. It is worth knowing the failure had no error message.

The amenity badges were never going to render. The code that reads content files mis-parsed indented lists, so every Location's amenity list came back as one empty entry. Both were fixed; the second was confirmed by running the old parser against the files as they were committed, which returned an empty list for all three Locations. Neither had reached a deployed site, because there is not one yet — which is the argument for the order of work, not against it.

BLOCK A

Stack, hosting and running cost

3 decisions + 1 from the build

The three decisions that are genuinely hard to undo later. Everything in blocks B to F sits on top of these.

ADR 0002

TinaCMS is the content system

Accepted
Why Staff log in by email — no GitHub account. Edits save as files in the repo, so the live site keeps serving even if the editing service is down, and Claude can make the same edits through chat. The alternative, Decap, lost its non-GitHub login path when Netlify deprecated Git Gateway in 2026.
What it costs The free tier is 2 editor logins. A third person is $24/mo, five is $41/mo — and neither includes an approval step. Assets are capped at 100 MB, which a real set of store photography exceeds. Reversing this is a CMS migration.
ADR 0003

Next.js and React, not Astro

Accepted
Why You asked for click-to-edit: open the page, click the words, change them, watch the preview. TinaCMS delivers that through a React hook. On Astro staff would get a form-based admin panel instead — good, but not click-to-edit. The framework was chosen by the editing experience, not by preference.
What it costs Every component carrying editable content is a client component. Reversing this is a rebuild, not a migration.
ADR 0013

Free tiers everywhere except Vercel

Accepted
Why Vercel's own fair-use terms restrict the free Hobby plan to non-commercial, personal use. This is a commercial retail business, so Vercel Pro at $20/mo is required by the terms of service rather than chosen for a feature. Running a commercial site on Hobby risks the project being paused — on a site whose job is posting fuel prices, that is not a $20 saving.
What it costs $20/mo, and no approval gate before a staff edit goes live. That trade is acceptable only because every change is a commit with an author and a one-click revert. Side benefit: per-minute promo scheduling was going to cost extra on Hobby and is now included.
FROM THE BUILD

Pages are prerendered; only the live parts are fetched per visitor

Settled 18 Sep · constrains every later change
Why The site has to be two contradictory things: instant everywhere, and current to the second in three specific places (the box below Block C lists them). The build resolves that by doing both — each page is assembled once and served from a cache near the visitor, while those three pieces are fetched fresh on every visit. It is what lets a pushed price be live immediately without making every page slow to load.
What it costs It is now a property of the code rather than an intention, which is the point of listing it here: the shape is fixed, and the three live pieces are the only ones. Anything added later that must also be current to the second is a change to this decision, not a free addition — and the site can never be flattened to static files, which is the failure the box below Block C describes.
BLOCK B

What the site is, and how people move through it

6 decisions

"Exceptional navigation" was one of the three must-haves. These six are the answer, and four of them were settled by measurement rather than by opinion.

ADR 0001

Only the three market pages come from lcc-lummi.com

Accepted · amended 17 Sep
Why The brief said "merge three sites", but lcc-lummi.com is the parent enterprise — casino, golf, billboards, the Salish Village development. Importing it would bury the retail brand inside a holding company.
What it costs The merge is deliberately partial. LCC appears once, as a single footer link. If those businesses ever need surfacing, that is a separate site. Amended 17 Sep: the Careers link tested this rule and you settled it — the rule is about mentions, not destinations, so an outbound link labelled by the job it does is fine and the label must stay "Careers", with no company name and no logo beside it.
ADR 0008

Three nav items: Home · Locations · Truck Stop

Accepted · amended 18 Sep
Why Fuel Prices came out because the prices are already on every page, in front of the guest, wherever they are. A nav item pointing at content the guest is currently looking at is spent budget. Three items also fit a phone without a hamburger menu; four were tight.
What it costs /fuel-prices still exists — it is the natural landing page for "gas prices near me" — so it must stay linked from somewhere. Amended 18 September: that is the price panel alone. A footer link was planned and dropped, because the prices are already on the page the guest is reading and the panel link sits directly under them. The page stays in the sitemap, so search still reaches it.
ADR 0006

The header sticks; the price rail does not

Accepted · amended
Why Location pages are long. A pinned header is what stops a guest getting lost. Pinning the price card under it was tried and broke the panel: after ~300px of scroll the open panel was painted correctly but was not there for the finger — tapping a price closed it. Reproduced 3 runs of 3.
What it costs On a phone the price card scrolls away with the page. On desktop it survives, because it condenses to a one-line bar whose panel is positioned against the screen instead of the card.
ADR 0009

Truck Stop leads the list, and a page never lists itself

Accepted
Why The truck stop is the flagship's distinguishing feature and the reason a whole class of guests is on the site. On the Exit 260 page, the Exit 260 card is dropped and the heading becomes "Our other locations". The Exit 260 card stays on the truck stop page, because they are two separate stores sharing one property.
What it costs The list is derived from the location data, never hand-placed per page. The grid must collapse the vacated column, or every Location page shows two cards and 288px of dead space.
ADR 0010

The phone footer runs two columns, at 44px per target

Accepted
Why One column was 515px — a screen and a half of scrolling past six words. Two columns makes it 324px. The obvious worry, that closer links get mistouched, was tested rather than argued: 22,568 simulated touches at a ±30px aim error produced 5.9% vertical errors and 0.0% horizontal ones. Not one crossed the gutter.
What it costs Footer links are padded anchors, not text — so the hit area cannot be lost by someone later restyling the text. The 14px row gap stays: a touch that does nothing beats a touch that navigates somewhere the guest did not ask for.
ADR 0011

Back-to-top appears only past 1.75 screens

Accepted
Why Measured across six built pages at four thresholds. At the obvious setting the button flickers into life for the final 3% of two pages — a control that arrives after the guest has already landed. At 0.75 of a screen it is generous where the page is long and silent everywhere else.
What it costs Today only Home-on-a-phone gets one, and that is the rule working rather than a feature missing. The footer reserves 74–78px for it instead of the button dodging.
BLOCK C

How pages and promotions get made

6 decisions

This is the block that answers must-have #1 — easy updates by non-technical staff. The short version: staff add pages and promotions without an engineer, and cannot break the site's furniture while doing it.

ADR 0015

A page is a document, not a hand-built route

Accepted
Why Every page type was originally hard-wired, which means every new page is an engineering ticket. Pages are now documents in four collections — general pages, the swappable main page, product info pages, and promotions. Adding a page is a content action.
What it costs The header, footer, waterline, price block and back-to-top belong to the layout and are not addressable from a page. A staff-made page cannot lose the furniture or move it — which is the point, but it also means genuinely new page types still need an engineer.
ADR 0018

Promotions are scheduled documents; pages compose them

Accepted
Why A promotion carries its own start and end date and a placement, so it appears and disappears on its own with no scheduler to maintain and no one to remember. Any number of them, on any page, in rows of one to four across; a row re-divides evenly when one expires.
What it costs Rows are capped at six per page. The whole-main-page swap is demoted to a redesign escape hatch and is never scheduled. This only works because pages are rendered per request — see the box below.
ADR 0016

Tenants get cards and pages; a tenant is never a Location

Accepted · you said yes, 17 Sep
Why Piroshky Piroshky, Wendy's, Black Bear Diner and Hi-Tide Coffee are independent businesses on the property. Filing one as a Location would put it in the Locations index, in the footer, and as a row in the fuel price table — a Wendy's with a dash under Regular. The index is called "Also at Exit 260", never "Salish Village".
What it costs The template renders the disclosure — "Independently owned and operated. Not part of Lummi Bay Market." — so it cannot be forgotten. No third-party logo renders without written permission, enforced by a checkbox in the schema. The Cove Kitchen stays an amenity, because Lummi Bay Market runs it.
ADR 0007

The promo region sits below the page title

Accepted · reversed on measurement
Why This ADR originally said "above the title" and the numbers reversed it. On an 812px phone, a promo above the title pushes the page title to 388px — 48% down the screen. A guest who tapped "Exit 260" would be half a screen in before the page said Exit 260. Below the title it sits at 190px and is still the first large graphic.
What it costs Each promo picks a width from a fixed ladder — full, two-thirds, half, third, quarter — so any mix tiles exactly. Free-typed percentages were rejected: 45% + 45% leaves a 10% orphan.
ADR 0023

One promo image, cropped five ways

Accepted · amended same day
Why Five slot shapes need five crops, or one clever master. A 2400 × 1350 file with the subject held inside the centre 2400 × 480 strip is never cropped sideways — every slot takes a full-width cut and trims only top and bottom. One upload serves all five desktop slots and the phone.
What it costs Whoever supplies promo art has to compose to that strip. Three text fields, everywhere — eyebrow, headline, button — and the headline is capped at 28 characters, counted for the editor as they type.
ADR 0022

One share card, 1200 × 630, with per-page overrides

Accepted · default already built
Why Every link pasted into Facebook, a text message or Slack unfurls into a card, and with nothing specified that card is whatever the platform scrapes. Three different crops are in the wild — WhatsApp cuts 285px off each side — so the logo and any words live inside a 570 × 510 safe box.
What it costs The file is opaque navy JPG under 200 KB, never a transparent PNG: several chat clients composite transparency onto black, which would put a navy wordmark on a black field. Per-Location cards are optional; the sitewide default already covers every link.

One technical constraint runs under three of these decisions

The site must never be built as a fully static export. Three separate features depend on pages being assembled at the moment a visitor asks for them:

  1. The emergency notice — published globally in under a second (ADR 0017).
  2. Fuel prices — live the instant they are saved, with no rebuild (ADR 0024).
  3. Holiday hours — evaluated against today's date, in Pacific time (ADR 0027).

This is worth a sentence in a business review because of how it fails: a static export would not throw an error. The three features would simply never fire, and nobody would find out until the morning one of them mattered. It is a launch check, not a footnote.

Built as described, 18 September. All three now work this way in code, and the constraint is written into the build in three separate places so that whoever meets it next meets it three times. It stays on the launch checklist regardless: the failure is silent, so the only way to know it has not happened is to look.

BLOCK D

The fuel price system

3 decisions

Must-have #2. Eight prices — regular and diesel at each of the three Locations, diesel and DEF at the Truck Stop.

ADR 0004

All eight prices in one form, and "all locations" is a button

Accepted
Why The proposal of a shared price plus per-location overrides was rejected, and the reason is a failure mode rather than a preference: check the box and all three show the shared value; uncheck it and they silently fall back to per-location numbers that may be weeks stale. Nobody edits the number that changes. On a posted fuel price that is a wrong number in front of a customer.
What it costs One price per place per grade is stored, always. The checkbox types into all three at once — it controls the keyboard, never what the site reads. The Truck Stop is always priced separately: truck-lane diesel is not car-lane diesel. Prices are not click-editable on their own page, because a CMS form covers one document.
ADR 0005

One price table, one component, three states

Accepted
Why One table with a column per grade, so a guest can see at a glance that one Location is three cents cheaper — invisible in a run of inline text. A place that does not sell a grade gets a dash, which is information rather than a gap. It rests as a card, condenses to a one-line bar as the page scrolls, and opens a panel for all prices.
What it costs This is the largest single component on the site and the one with the most decisions behind it. The card is capped at 400px at both widths, and it overlays page content when it opens rather than pushing it down.
ADR 0024

A posted price is live the moment it is saved

Accepted · your call, 16 Sep
Why You said it plainly: once a price is pushed it must go live immediately. So the page is prebuilt and fast, and only the price block is assembled per visit, reading the content service directly. There is no rebuild to wait for and no cache to clear, because no price was ever baked into the page.
What it costs If that service is slow or unreachable, the block shows the last known price and its timestamp rather than an error or a blank — a gap where a number belongs reads as a broken site. The "updated" stamp ships and is not optional: with publishing instant, it is now the only thing on the page telling a guest how fresh the number is. The remaining lag is human: if the pump changes at 6am and nobody opens the CMS until 9am, the site is wrong for three hours, and no architecture reaches that.
BLOCK E

Brand, artwork and legibility

4 decisions

The logo itself is locked and none of these touch it. One decision in this block is a rejection — something was built, shown, and turned down.

ADR 0012

The illustrated art layer was built and rejected

Rejected · 25 Aug
Why Wave waterlines, a shoreline band, per-Location motifs and a paddle mark were built, reviewed and turned down. They have been removed. What survives is the placement rule, which was never the problem, and two defects worth not rediscovering.
What it costs The site has no illustration or photography today; the waterline is a plain colour bar. That is also the one thing making a fast launch possible — the current design does not depend on any artwork that has yet to be commissioned or culturally approved.
ADR 0014

Cedar and teal are graphic colours, not text colours

Accepted
Why Contrast had never been measured on this project — tap targets were measured to the pixel and colour not at all. Measured, three places already failed. Cedar carries text only at 24px or above; teal never carries text on a light ground.
What it costs The "Get the App" pill changes from cedar fill to bone fill with navy text. Cedar at 2.43:1 on the navy header and navy at 2.20:1 on cedar are both under the 3:1 a control's own edge requires — which is why every pill carries a fine bone ring. That ring is an accessibility requirement, not a style, and the two pills that had drifted apart have been corrected to match. Cedar also never takes opacity: at 75% it measures 2.34 and fails even as large text.
ADR 0020

The sasquatch backdrop, with a switch that turns him off

Accepted · 27 Aug
Why A subtle figure held behind the page as it scrolls. It is built as content, not code: image, on/off, opacity, side and height are all CMS fields, so swapping the art or switching it off takes one checkbox and is live in under a second — no engineer, no deploy.
What it costs The measured ceiling is 10% opacity, because the supplied artwork is a rendered figure whose fur is darker than any brand colour, and the dim grey label is the text it threatens first. Above 12% that label fails contrast. The field is not clamped — the owner sets it — but the schema shows the line and warns past it. Any page needing a clean ground opts out.
ADR 0021

Motifs may sit in the header, under four rules

Accepted · 15 Sep
Why This replaced a flat prohibition. Art may sit in the header, right-aligned, one row, no closer than 20px to the right edge of the navigation, masked over a brand colour. Each rule prevents a failure that has already happened in this build.
What it costs Art behind the navigation is out — measured, it eats the contrast of the bone nav text, and the active item starts with most of its headroom already spent. The band is decorative: hidden from screen readers, and hidden entirely for visitors using high-contrast settings.
BLOCK F

Privacy, third parties, and things that change on a schedule

6 decisions + 1 from the build

The first three are one position held consistently: the site sets no cookies, so there is nothing to consent to. That is a promise printed in the footer, which makes it a standing constraint on everything added later.

ADR 0025

No cookie banner, because there is nothing to consent to

Accepted · amended by 0026
Why Stronger than deciding a banner is not required: it makes the legal question moot rather than answered. A banner on a site that sets no cookies is theatre every guest has to dismiss. The footer says so in plain words — "No cookies. Visits counted anonymously." — which a sceptic can verify in ten seconds.
What it costs That sentence is a checkable claim the build has to keep true, so it constrains every future embed. Analytics is Vercel's — cookieless, already included in the $20, one line of code. Google Analytics was declined: it would not be illegal, but it costs exactly this position, and it undercounts by 10–30% anyway. The one thing that would reopen it is Google Ads.
ADR 0026

A /privacy page, required by the app rather than the website

Accepted · text supplied 17 Sep
Why Neither app store will publish the Rewards app without a public policy URL. The page keeps the footer sentence and adds the link, because they are different kinds of statement: one is a specific checkable fact about this website, the other a document that mostly describes the app. The page opens with a short, labelled website section so the two never appear to contradict each other.
What it costs Once that URL is typed into a store listing it can never be renamed or taken down. Your policy text is in the repo verbatim and carries three things for counsel to look at — including a clause about raffle costs that appears to have come from a sweepstakes document. It was left exactly as supplied: quietly rewording a policy changes what people already agreed to.
ADR 0028

Social links only — never an embedded feed

Accepted · your call, 17 Sep
Why A plain link to Facebook sets nothing and loads nothing. A feed widget, a follow button or an official share button loads third-party code and sets cookies on arrival, before anyone clicks — which would make the sentence in the footer directly above it false.
What it costs No feeds, no follow or like buttons, no follower counts, ever. The test is plain enough to apply without a lawyer: does anything load from the platform before the visitor clicks? The accounts are a CMS list, and an empty list renders nothing — no placeholder icons, so nothing half-finished can survive to launch.
ADR 0019

One map on /contact with three pins

Accepted · amended by 0025
Why One map beats three. Built in Google My Maps and embedded — free, no API key, exact control. Three pins, not four: the Truck Stop shares Exit 260's address, so a fourth would land on top of the third and its pin note says it is there.
What it costs Two hard conditions. The map must be set to public or the embed renders nothing for visitors, silently. And it must be created in a company Google account from the start — a map in a personal account leaves when that person does, and ownership transfer on consumer Google products is not reliable. The page shows a static map with Directions links until a guest clicks to load the interactive one.
ADR 0017

An emergency notice, live in under a second

Accepted
Why Weather, a closure, a road. One switch turns on a bar that rides inside the pinned header on every page, so it cannot be scrolled past. It publishes globally in under a second with no rebuild. When it is off, nothing renders — no empty bar, no reserved space.
What it costs Somebody has to be able to publish it at 5am. There are two editor logins; if both belong to people who are asleep or whose laptop is at the office, the mechanism does not work when it is needed. It is editable from a phone browser, and the person who would actually be doing it should test that once, in the dark, before launch. Taking it down matters as much: a stale storm notice on a sunny Tuesday costs more credibility than never posting one.
ADR 0027

A second hours line, on a date window

Accepted · your call, 17 Sep
Why Normal hours stay one line and are always there. Each Location can carry a temporary second line with a start and end date — so New Year's Eve hours are typed in November and revert on their own. It fixes the hours everywhere they appear at once: the cards, the index, the detail page and /contact all read the same value.
What it costs The end date is required. "Until further notice" has to be written as a date, because an override with no end is a permanently wrong hours line waiting to happen — and it fails silently, since the page still shows a perfectly plausible time. There is deliberately no on/off toggle: a toggle is something someone must come back and switch off, which is the exact failure this field exists to prevent. Dates are whole days in Pacific time, so an override never flips the afternoon before.
FROM THE BUILD

Three locks on /privacy, because losing it costs the app

Settled 18 Sep · raised by a near-miss
Why Both app stores hold the Rewards listing against a working public policy URL, so /privacy is the one page on this site whose absence is an outage somewhere else. It is now protected three ways: the CMS cannot delete the page, its address is fixed by the filename rather than a field anyone can edit, and it is forced into the sitemap and out of the hide-from-search setting in code — so it cannot be hidden by a checkbox on a page nobody is watching.
What it costs The page is deliberately harder to change than every other page, and the text inside it is harder still: policy wording is replaced wholesale by whoever wrote it, never tidied in passing. One clause in the current text is still counsel's to resolve, and the date at the bottom of the page moves only when they hand over new words — it is not a field to keep current. A policy page that has been improved by a well-meaning editor is worse than a stale one.

What is still open

Answered by the client, 17 September 2026 — all twelve, plus three follow-ups the same day.

Nothing on this list blocks the build any more. Six items are closed outright — two of them (A8, A9) on a second pass the same day, after the first answers raised a correction and a rule question. The rest were not closed but moved: artwork, promotions, copy, amenity lists and the map all shift into a content pass done after the build, in TinaCMS or a chat session, and the build runs on placeholders. That is a real decision with a real cost and it is named in the table rather than buried — deferring words and pictures does not make them smaller, it makes them invisible until the site is public.

One item has been added to this list since, and it came from the build rather than from you: the CMS account. It was never asked about because it was assumed to exist, and it does not. It blocks no page and no deadline, but it blocks the one test that proves the site does what it was commissioned to do, so it now heads the asks at the foot of this page.

The one change worth reading twice: the Rewards app is already published in both stores. That retires a worry recorded in Block F — that /privacy gated the app submission. It never could have; the submission already happened.

What two weeks would buy

Short answer: a complete, working site, live, with placeholder imagery and first-draft words. Everything that is engineering fits in two weeks. Nothing that needs a third party — artwork, cultural approval, an app submission, finished copy — does, and no amount of engineering speed changes that.

This plan is now partly a progress report. The rows are marked with where the build actually got to, so the remaining estimate is a shorter read than the original one. The honest summary: the parts that are pure engineering went faster than the plan, and the two rows that are not — the day-4 gate and the cut-over — have not moved at all, because neither is waiting on engineering.

The reason it is possible at all is the rejection in Block E. Because the illustrated art layer was removed, the current design does not depend on any artwork that has not been approved — so launching on schedule does not mean shipping placeholder Coast Salish motifs, which would break a hard rule. It means launching clean and adding art when it is ready.

That is a reasonable way to launch — it is not the same as launching finished, and it is worth agreeing to deliberately rather than discovering on the day.

What a two-week launch actually asks of you

Three things, and the first is new. Building the site turned one assumption into an ask: the CMS needs an account in the business's name before anyone can log into it, and until that exists the day-4 gate cannot be run at all — which means the two must-haves the whole project is for are, today, untested.

  1. Open the TinaCMS account for the business and hand over the two keys it issues. It is the free tier, so this costs nothing and takes about ten minutes, but nobody else can do it: it has to be a company account, not an engineer's. Everything about editing is blocked behind it — logging in, the two seats, click-to-edit, and the fuel price test below.
  2. Name the two people who get CMS logins (A7) before day 4, so the gate above is a real test with a real person rather than a demo. Now second in line behind the account, not because it got less urgent but because it cannot happen first.
  3. Check the registrar logins (A6) whenever it suits. Deferred, correctly, since nothing in the build touches DNS — but it is the only open item that can add weeks rather than days, and it costs an afternoon to rule out.

Everything else follows the site live. Copy, photography, artwork, promotions and tenant records all land through the CMS afterwards without an engineer — which is what Block C was for, and it is now the plan of record rather than a capability held in reserve.
